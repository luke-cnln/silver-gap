The Crown counted taxes, not silver

The royal accounts of Spanish America are usually read as a record of how much silver the mines produced. Set against an independent check, the mercury the mines used, they record something narrower: what the Crown managed to register and tax. This site follows the silver from the mountain to Europe and shows where it slipped out of the count.

Part one

Reading the ledger against the mines

Brading and Cross set the Seville registers beside a floor for production built from mercury consumption. Scroll through the six steps, then explore the chart yourself.

Step 1 of 6

The Crown's count

Silver registered on arrival at Seville, five years at a time (Hamilton's series). It peaks at 58.2 million pesos in 1591–95 and falls to 5.6 million by 1656–60. Read on its own, this is the famous seventeenth-century collapse.

Production could only be rebuilt from the royal fifth, the mint registers and registered shipments, all "collected for taxation purposes" (Brading and Cross 1972, 568–70).

Step 2 of 6

An independent floor

Mercury was an industrial input, not a taxed product. Multiply the mercury the refineries used by the silver it yields and you get the least silver they can have produced: "the one reliable check upon the veracity of the fiscal data" (Brading and Cross 1972, 570).

It is a floor, not an estimate. It misses all silver smelted rather than amalgamated.

Step 3 of 6

The lines part

Until about 1620 the two series move together. From the 1620s the register keeps falling while the floor holds between 30 and 50 million. The shaded area is silver the mines produced that the Seville register never shows.

Step 4 of 6

Raising the floor

Brading and Cross add 15 per cent for smelted silver and 10 per cent for gold. The adjusted floor sits higher again, and it is still a minimum: contraband ore refined outside the official system is only partly inside it.

Step 5 of 6

1651–55: 12.1 against 62.1

In the worst quinquennium the register records 12.1 million pesos against an adjusted floor of 62.1 million. Because the floor is a minimum, the Crown's share of real output was at most about 19 per cent.

Step 6 of 6

A Spanish crisis, not an American one

Hamilton's register ends in 1660. The floor continues and only dips sharply after 1680, which is when Brading and Cross date Peru's real decline. The crisis of the mid-century was "first and foremost a Spanish crisis" (Brading and Cross 1972, 576).

Part three

Four points where silver left the count

Follow a peso from the Cerro Rico to a European counting house. Each station has its own sources, and each source carries its own doubt.

Ore shares at Potosí

Wage labour at Potosí was paid partly in ore. Workers kept shares of rich ore (corpa), mined at weekends and carried ore out at the end of shifts, selling it at the Indian ore market for small-scale refining (Smith 2008, 36). This silver avoided the Crown's 20 per cent tax and was tolerated because owners needed the labour (37).

Less certainty: not all of it escaped

Tandeter notes trapiche silver was also sold to the state bank (1981, 134). Informal mining did not always mean uncounted silver, so these shares cannot simply be added to the official totals.

Tolerated, not stopped

Mexico used troops to end ore shares in the 1760s–70s. At Potosí owners chose tolerance for fear of losing workers (Tandeter 1981, 135).

Late evidence for an early question

The 1759 and 1774 figures are eighteenth-century. They show the scale informal production could reach, not its exact size in the 1650s.

Tax receipts are not output

Garner's long-run series, the standard reconstruction, is built from treasury tax receipts and converted into production estimates in several steps (Garner 1988, 900 n. 4). He is plain about what it misses: silver never registered "would not appear in the treasury ledgers", and filling the gaps could soften the seventeenth-century contraction (903 n. 9).

Two official counts disagree

Totals from mint registrations run higher, and grow faster, than totals from tax receipts. Exemptions cut treasury takings but not mint entries.

Garner 1988, 905 n.

Missing ledgers

Not all known treasury ledgers have been consulted. Contraband and missing accounts could reshape the seventeenth-century curve.

Garner 1988, 903 n. 9

A price that pulled silver west

Silver was worth about twice as much in China as in Spain (Flynn and Giráldez 1995, 206). That premium pulled it across the Pacific and down the Río de la Plata, outside the Seville trade.

Official complicity

The leak was not only evasion of the state. Viceroy Cañete invested in a China voyage himself, and Montesclaros admitted that custom had overridden prohibition (Bonialian 2022, 49, 53 ⚑). At Buenos Aires, Brading and Cross point to "official laxity" (561).

Seville against the gazettes

The classic series of American treasure reaching Europe, Hamilton's and the Chaunus', come from the Seville registers and duties, which covered only the taxed trade (Flynn and Giráldez 1995, 205 n. 7). Morineau rebuilt arrivals from Dutch gazettes and French consular reports and found no seventeenth-century collapse: the peak of the 1590s was matched in the 1650s and then surpassed (Parker 1986, 482–83).

The counter-count is uncertain too

MacLeod calls the gazettes "only well-educated guesses" for 1660–1720 (1989, 166). Replacing the Crown's number with Morineau's would repeat the mistake in the other direction.

Part six

A range, not a number

Verdict

The royal accounts are a record of the Crown's reach, not of the mines' output. The gap is systematic, it widens at every step from mine to market, and it can be bounded but not closed.

The numbers can tell us the least silver produced (from mercury), the share the Crown registered, and when the two diverged.

They cannot tell us a single true production figure. Corpa was partly sold back to the state bank, gazette figures are estimates, and Bonialian's figures are contemporary claims, some of them partisan.

So the seventeenth-century "crisis" of American silver was in large part a crisis of registration.
